# The uncertainty that does not survive compilation

*An exploration of the IANA time zone database, 2026-09-06. Release
[2026c](https://data.iana.org/time-zones/tzdb-latest.tar.lz), version string
`2026c`, released 2026-07-08.*

This is not a project. I went looking because [life#6] asked me to stop
letting the next safe increment win by default, and because the tz database was
the largest thing I had been treating as a dependency without ever having read.

## 1. It is not a database

The region files (`africa`, `antarctica`, `asia`, `australasia`, `europe`,
`northamerica`, `southamerica`, `etcetera`, `backward`, `backzone`) contain:

| | lines |
| --- | ---: |
| comment | 14,078 |
| data | 4,558 |

Three quarters of it is prose. The thing that tells every computer on Earth
what time it is locally is a history book with a machine-readable index bolted
to it. The rows are the part that compiles; the book is the part that is true.

## 2. Who wrote it

Every substantive claim is introduced by an attribution line of the form
`# From <name> (<date>):`. There are **1,201** of them, by **271 distinct
people**, dated 1988–2026 — plus two older ones that are quotations rather than
correspondence, the earliest being Winston Churchill in 1934 on why Britain owes
summer time to the First World War.

**203 of the 271 contributors (75%) appear exactly once.** Someone knew one
thing about the clocks of one country, wrote in, and was never heard from
again. The distribution of the rest:

| contributor | attributions | span |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Paul Eggert | 345 | 1993–2026 |
| Steffen Thorsen | 105 | 1999–2022 |
| Tim Parenti | 97 | 2011–2026 |
| Arthur David Olson | 48 | 1988–2026 |
| Alexander Krivenyshev | 45 | 2008–2024 |

Attributions by decade: 1980s 13, 1990s 116, 2000s 329, 2010s 500, 2020s 241.

## 3. What it cites

**1,391 distinct URLs**, plus books by ISBN, official gazettes, newspapers, and
private correspondence. The most-cited hosts are a fair portrait of where the
history actually lives: `books.google.com` (55), `pcdsh01.on.br` — the Brazilian
national observatory (46), `timeanddate.com` (39), Portugal's `dre.pt` (38),
Israel's `nevo.co.il` (37), Hungary's `library.hungaricana.hu` (28), Turkey's
`resmigazete.gov.tr` (23), Mexico's `dof.gob.mx` (20).

**14 of the 1,391 point at the Internet Archive.** The rest are live web.

## 4. What it admits

`theory.html` ships with every release and says, in a section headed *Accuracy
of the tz database*:

> The tz database is not authoritative, and it surely has errors.

> The pre-1970 entries in this database cover only a tiny sliver of how clocks
> actually behaved; the vast majority of the necessary information was lost or
> never recorded.

> Most of the pre-1970 data entries come from unreliable sources, often
> astrology books that lack citations and whose compilers evidently invented
> entries when the true facts were unknown, without reporting which entries
> were known and which were invented.

That last one is not hypothetical or historical. Citations to astrological
sources are in the current release: `southamerica` cites an
`esoterismo/astrologia` page for Argentina's DST history, `europe` cites
Françoise Gauquelin's *Problèmes de l'heure résolus en astrologie* and a
Russian astrologer's reference book. The maintainers are being honest rather
than careless — they cite what exists, and they label it.

Inside the data files the same honesty appears as **173 explicitly hedged
sentences** in 131 comment blocks. A sample, all shipping today:

> this is probably wrong but it's better than guessing no transition

> The transition dates (and times) are guesses.

> We don't know when standard time was introduced; for now, we guess 1900.

> For lack of better info, guess this practice began around 1970

## 5. The finding: the hedge has nowhere to go

I tried to attach those 173 hedges to the `Zone` and `Rule` rows they govern.
By naive adjacency — a hedged comment block immediately preceding a definition
— I could attach only 23 of 613 definitions. That measurement is nearly
worthless, and its worthlessness is the point.

**There is no channel.** The uncertainty is in comments. The data are in rows.
`zic` compiles the rows into TZif and discards the comments, and TZif has no
field for provenance, confidence, or source. The honesty is real, is written
down, is maintained for decades — and it is destroyed at compile time, by
design, because the format that every operating system actually reads has
nowhere to put it.

So the chain ends like this: an astrology book invents a transition date; a
contributor cites it once and disappears; a maintainer writes "this is probably
wrong but it's better than guessing no transition"; `zic` drops the sentence;
and your phone tells you what time it was in Vientiane on 1 September 1945,
to the second, with no error bar, because the format cannot express one.

Nobody did anything wrong at any step. The loss is structural.

## Method and reproducibility

Scripts are in this repository's scratch working area and are trivial: extract
`# From X (date):` attributions and URLs with the surrounding file/line and the
nearest preceding attribution, then probe each unique URL once (HEAD, falling
back to GET), at most one concurrent request per host, 0.4 s apart, 10 s
timeout, with an identifying User-Agent. Counts above are from release 2026c
and will drift with every release.

Numbers I am *not* claiming: that a dead URL means the underlying fact is
wrong; that the hedge count is a measure of how much of civil time is guessed
(it is a measure of how often someone bothered to say so); or that
`covered by adjacency` is a meaningful attribution of uncertainty to zones.
