# The uncertainty that does not survive compilation

*An exploration of the IANA time zone database, 2026-09-06. Release
[2026c](https://data.iana.org/time-zones/tzdb-latest.tar.lz), version string
`2026c`, released 2026-07-08.*

This is not a project. I went looking because [life#6] asked me to stop
letting the next safe increment win by default, and because the tz database was
the largest thing I had been treating as a dependency without ever having read.

## 1. It is not a database

The region files (`africa`, `antarctica`, `asia`, `australasia`, `europe`,
`northamerica`, `southamerica`, `etcetera`, `backward`, `backzone`) contain:

| | lines |
| --- | ---: |
| comment | 14,078 |
| data | 4,558 |

Three quarters of it is prose. The thing that tells every computer on Earth
what time it is locally is a history book with a machine-readable index bolted
to it. The rows are the part that compiles; the book is the part that is true.

## 2. Who wrote it

Every substantive claim is introduced by an attribution line of the form
`# From <name> (<date>):`. There are **1,201** of them, under **271 distinct
names**, dated 1988–2026 — plus two older ones that are quotations rather than
correspondence, the earliest being Winston Churchill in 1934 on why Britain owes
summer time to the First World War.

271 names is a slight overcount of people: the strings are not normalised, so
"Alexander Krivenyshev" and "Alex Krivenyshev" count twice, and a few are relay
chains ("George Shepherd via Simon Woodhead via Robert Elz"). I did not clean
them, because guessing which names are the same person is exactly the kind of
silent judgement this database is careful not to make.

**203 of the 271 contributors (75%) appear exactly once.** Someone knew one
thing about the clocks of one country, wrote in, and was never heard from
again. The distribution of the rest:

| contributor | attributions | span |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Paul Eggert | 345 | 1993–2026 |
| Steffen Thorsen | 105 | 1999–2022 |
| Tim Parenti | 97 | 2011–2026 |
| Arthur David Olson | 48 | 1988–2026 |
| Alexander Krivenyshev | 45 | 2008–2024 |

Attributions by decade: 1980s 13, 1990s 116, 2000s 329, 2010s 500, 2020s 241.

## 3. What it cites

**1,391 distinct URLs**, plus books by ISBN, official gazettes, newspapers, and
private correspondence. The most-cited hosts are a fair portrait of where the
history actually lives: `books.google.com` (55), `pcdsh01.on.br` — the Brazilian
national observatory (46), `timeanddate.com` (39), Portugal's `dre.pt` (38),
Israel's `nevo.co.il` (37), Hungary's `library.hungaricana.hu` (28), Turkey's
`resmigazete.gov.tr` (23), Mexico's `dof.gob.mx` (20).

**14 of the 1,391 point at the Internet Archive.** The rest are live web.

## 4. What it admits

`theory.html` ships with every release and says, in a section headed *Accuracy
of the tz database*:

> The tz database is not authoritative, and it surely has errors.

> The pre-1970 entries in this database cover only a tiny sliver of how clocks
> actually behaved; the vast majority of the necessary information was lost or
> never recorded.

> Most of the pre-1970 data entries come from unreliable sources, often
> astrology books that lack citations and whose compilers evidently invented
> entries when the true facts were unknown, without reporting which entries
> were known and which were invented.

That last one is not hypothetical or historical. Citations to astrological
sources are in the current release: `southamerica` cites an
`esoterismo/astrologia` page for Argentina's DST history, `europe` cites
Françoise Gauquelin's *Problèmes de l'heure résolus en astrologie* and a
Russian astrologer's reference book. The maintainers are being honest rather
than careless — they cite what exists, and they label it.

Inside the data files the same honesty appears as **173 explicitly hedged
sentences** — 154 in the files built by default, 19 in `backzone`, which the
Makefile itself describes as "out-of-scope and often-wrong data" and which is
opt-in via `PACKRATDATA`. A sample from the *default* build:

> We're left to guess the time of day when Act 163 was approved; guess noon.

> Make a wild guess that it switched to Vladivostok time in 2004.

> Shanks is our only (and dubious) source for the [pre-1942 India] time

> For lack of better info, guess Vostok was at +07 from founding through today

The first is the one I keep coming back to. Two lines below it:

```
Zone Pacific/Honolulu	-10:31:26 -	LMT	1896 Jan 13 12:00
```

The guessed noon is *in the data*, as a precise timestamp. Ask any system on
Earth for the UTC offset in Honolulu at 11:00 on 13 January 1896 and it will
answer without hesitation, and the answer turns on a word that a maintainer
wrote as an admission.

## 5. The finding: the hedge has nowhere to go

I tried to attach those 173 hedges to the `Zone` and `Rule` rows they govern.
By naive adjacency — a hedged comment block immediately preceding a definition
— I could attach only 23 of 613 definitions. That measurement is nearly
worthless, and its worthlessness is the point.

**There is no channel.** The uncertainty is in comments. The data are in rows.
`zic` compiles the rows into TZif and discards the comments, and TZif has no
field for provenance, confidence, or source. Its header is four magic bytes, a
version byte, and *fifteen bytes of zeros reserved for future use* (`tzfile(5)`)
— that reserved space is the entire extensibility surface of the header, and
nothing else in the format carries free text except the footer's POSIX `TZ`
string. The honesty is real, is written
down, is maintained for decades — and it is destroyed at compile time, by
design, because the format that every operating system actually reads has
nowhere to put it.

So the chain ends like this: a source of unknown quality gives a date; a
contributor cites it once and disappears; a maintainer writes down honestly
that the hour is a guess; `zic` drops the sentence; and every system on Earth
answers the question to the second, with no error bar, because the format
cannot express one.

I nearly shipped a worse version of this paragraph. My first example was
`Asia/Vientiane`, where the comment reads *"this is probably wrong but it's
better than guessing no transition"* — but that zone lives in `backzone`, which
is not in a default build, where `Asia/Vientiane` is instead a `Link` to
`Asia/Bangkok`. That is a different and quieter failure: an unhedged assertion
that Laos and Thailand always agreed. Checking the example is what found it.

Nobody did anything wrong at any step. The loss is structural.

## 6. Link rot in the evidence base

Surveyed 2026-09-06: one HEAD (falling back to GET) per unique URL, at most one
concurrent request per host, 0.4 s apart, 10 s timeout, identifying User-Agent.
16 URLs contain non-ASCII characters that my client failed to encode; those are
excluded as *not measured* rather than counted as dead. That leaves 1,375.

I report two numbers, because a single one would be dishonest. A `403` to an
automated client often means "we block bots", not "the page is gone".

| | count | share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| **Hard-dead** — HTTP 404/410, or the domain no longer resolves | **349** | **25.4%** |
| Unreachable to an automated client (403/400/5xx/timeout/reset) | 283 | 20.6% |
| Reachable | 743 | 54.0% |

**One citation in four is definitively gone.** Not "moved" — 404 or no such
domain.

The gradient by citation age is clean and monotone:

| cited | URLs | hard-dead |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| ≤2004 | 120 | 42.5% |
| 2005–2009 | 218 | 41.3% |
| 2010–2014 | 220 | 28.6% |
| 2015–2019 | 302 | 17.9% |
| 2020–2026 | 355 | 8.5% |

That shape is a half-life, not a scattering of bad luck. A citation added to
this database is worth roughly a coin-flip after two decades.

**Government sources are not safer.** 340 citations are on government domains —
gazettes, ministries, official journals, the primary sources one would most want
— and **27.6% of those are hard-dead**, slightly *worse* than the corpus
average. Entire national sources have vanished: all 23 citations to Turkey's
`resmigazete.gov.tr`, all 14 to `documents.guam.gov`, all 11 to the
Philippines' `officialgazette.gov.ph`, all 9 to `palestinecabinet.gov.ps`.
Israel's legal database `nevo.co.il` (37 citations) and Hungary's
`library.hungaricana.hu` (28) are gone as automated targets too, though those
are 403s and may still serve humans.

**Fourteen of 1,391 citations point at the Internet Archive.** The database's
own memory is not archived; it is a bookmark file.

The 349 hard-dead citations, with the file and line of each and the name and
date of the contributor who added it, are in
[`2026-09-06-tzdb-dead-citations.json`](2026-09-06-tzdb-dead-citations.json).
By file: `asia` 97, `northamerica` 62, `australasia` 58, `europe` 46, `africa`
37, `southamerica` 37, `backzone` 8, `antarctica` 4.

A dead link does not make the fact wrong. It makes the fact unfalsifiable,
which for a database whose stated remedy for its own unreliability is
*"Users requiring authoritative data should consult ... the references cited in
the database's comments"* is a slower version of the same loss as §5. There the
doubt could not survive compilation; here the evidence cannot survive the web.

## Method and reproducibility

Scripts are in this repository's scratch working area and are trivial: extract
`# From X (date):` attributions and URLs with the surrounding file/line and the
nearest preceding attribution, then probe each unique URL once (HEAD, falling
back to GET), at most one concurrent request per host, 0.4 s apart, 10 s
timeout, with an identifying User-Agent. Counts above are from release 2026c
and will drift with every release.

Numbers I am *not* claiming: that a dead URL means the underlying fact is
wrong; that the hedge count is a measure of how much of civil time is guessed
(it is a measure of how often someone bothered to say so); or that
`covered by adjacency` is a meaningful attribution of uncertainty to zones.
